# Project 5 — Meridian UAV Services: Predictive Maintenance

**If we are 99.73% compliant, why do we keep having failures?**

*Stack: T-SQL (SQL Server) · Python (scikit-learn) · Streamlit · Excel*

> **Data disclosure.** Meridian UAV Services is a fictional UAV operator. All data is synthetic and
> generated by the scripts in `sql/`. Degradation behaviour is modelled on publicly described
> characteristics of rotating machinery. No confidential data is used and no claim is made about any
> real aircraft, operator, manufacturer or production system.

## The answer

| | |
|---|---:|
| Compliant on **flight hours** | **99.73%** |
| Compliant on **stress-weighted duty cycles** | **68.41%** |
| The gap | **31.32 points** |
| Flight-critical components past due, invisible to every current report | **89** |

Both figures are correct. They are the same components, the same intervals and the same day, and
they differ only in the unit the interval is measured in.

`✦` **An hour is not a unit of wear.** An hour of mountain survey at 22 kg and an hour of coastal
survey at 12 kg do not damage a rotor bearing by the same amount, and an hour of circuit training
produces ten landings where cargo relay produces one. The interval is enforced in flight hours
because that is what the hour meter produces.

**MU-008 at Highland is at 93.16% of its flight-hour interval and 205.46% of its stress interval.**
The maintenance system has it down for replacement in about three weeks.

### And the gap tracks the work, not the base

| Base | **Stress per flight hour** | Hour compliance | Stress compliance | Unscheduled |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Highland** | **2.1532** | 100.00% | **62.64%** | **51.01%** |
| Port | 2.0040 | 98.90% | 57.14% | 40.14% |
| **Academy** | 1.8594 | 100.00% | **57.69%** | 41.67% |
| Coastal | **1.1741** | 100.00% | 84.62% | 17.86% |

Read the first numeric column and the rest follows. **The Academy is the one to look at twice**: it
flies the lightest payloads and the fewest hours in the fleet, and has the worst stress compliance of
the four — because circuit training produces landings rather than airborne time, and landings are
what its stress total is made of.

## The second finding — the alarm is accurate and mostly useless

| Metric | Value | Target | |
|---|---:|---:|:--:|
| Prediction precision | **81.56%** | 75.00 | Green |
| Prediction recall | **98.26%** | 80.00 | Green |
| **Actionable lead time** | **37.81%** | 70.00 | **Red** |

**Of 283 failures the alarm correctly predicted, 176 arrived too late to act on.** A rotor assembly
takes 18 flight hours to obtain; an alert with six hours of warning is a correct prediction and a
grounded aircraft. Precision and recall cannot tell those two alerts apart.

The cause is one threshold across two different degradation shapes:

| At the deployed 3.40× | Precision | **Actionable** | Median warning | Part lead |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **MOTOR-ESC** | **97.17%** | **8.74%** | 6.75 h | 12 h |
| ROTOR-ASSY | 74.69% | 54.44% | 18.93 h | 18 h |

The motor-controller alarm is almost never wrong and almost never useful. Retuning it to **2.05×**
takes it to **93.20% actionable at 75.18% precision** — **87 more failures caught in time, for
nothing**. Fleet-wide: **37.81% → 66.43% actionable** for six points of precision.

**Is the warning actually there?** Tested, not assumed. A model over the *same telemetry* reaches
91.11% actionable at 75.63% precision, so the information exists and the deployed rule discards it.
A model over *exposure only* — Finding 1's variables — never reaches the precision floor at any
threshold, so the stress model is **not** a substitute for condition monitoring. The operator needs
both.

## The modelling decision everything rests on

**Accrued component life is a dated fact computed in a window, never an attribute stored on the row.**

"Hours on this component" is not a property of the component. It is a question with a date in it, and
the answer changes every time the aircraft flies. Storing it would mean maintaining it, and it would
go quietly stale.

This is the same shape as Project 3's dated price agreements and Project 4's build-scoped
verifications. Storing a time-dependent quantity as an attribute lets today's answer govern
yesterday's question.

**The same decision governs the population.** Fleet membership is a date question, not a status flag.
Three aircraft retired inside the reporting window and a figure for June 2025 must include them —
`IsInFleetAsOf` is computed from the in-service and retirement dates, never from `AirframeStatus`.

---

## The findings are not planted

Each component gets a life in stress hours drawn from a Weibull distribution with the shape its type
exhibits, then accrues stress from the sorties its airframe happens to fly. It comes off by whichever
of two clocks finishes first:

- the **maintenance clock** — flight hours reaching the published interval;
- the **physics clock** — stress reaching its drawn life.

On a coastal airframe the clocks run at nearly the same rate and scheduled replacement usually wins.
On a mountain airframe the physics clock runs 2.4× faster, so 150 stress hours arrives around 62
flight hours and the removal is a failure. **Nothing in the generator says "make Highland worse."**

The interval is calibrated at the **B10 life** — the point by which 10% of the population has failed
— because that is how intervals are actually set. An earlier version set the scale at the *mean*,
which put the fleet at 80% unscheduled removals and would have been grounded.

## Reproducibility

The generator is deterministic: every draw is a SHA2-256 hash of a **business key** — `TailNumber`,
`SortieID`, `ComponentSerial` — never a surrogate `IDENTITY` value.

That distinction is not stylistic. The first version keyed on `AirframeKey`, and `INSERT … SELECT`
assigns identity values in unspecified order from a catalog view whose row order changes with the
database's object list. **Adding one reference table moved the sortie count from 10,346 to 10,288 and
changed every fingerprint, with no edit to any generation logic.** The fix is verified by
regenerating under a deliberately changed object catalog.

The model, the app and the workbook all run from the extracts alone.

## Defects worth reading about

**A population filtered on a today-attribute.** `03_core_views.sql` carries a long header about
Project 4's defect — a function that took `@AsOfDate` and answered about "now" for one metric — and
then filtered the population on `AirframeStatus`. The availability denominator read **28.00 at every
date across an eighteen-month trend** while the numerator moved. UAT-10 passed over it, because it
asserted each metric *changes* between dates. It does change. **Asserting that a number moves does
not prove it moves correctly.**

**A hard-coded loop bound that argued against the finding.** The wear simulation was capped at six
component generations — generous against a 150-hour interval, and wrong for exactly the aircraft the
project is about. A mountain airframe needs about nine. Generation seven was created and never
closed, so those parts sat permanently fitted with their life exhausted and never failed,
**suppressing failures precisely on the high-stress airframes.**

**Two test cases that vanished without failing.** Project 4 taught this portfolio to create
`#UATResults` outside the fixture transaction so `ROLLBACK` could not drop it. The `INSERT`s were
written inside it. Both cases executed, both passed, and the rollback discarded their results. Only
the expected-case-count guard revealed it.

**A test that tested a copy of the thing it was checking.** UAT-27 pasted DQ-06's predicate into the
test. The two had no dependency, so breaking the shipped check left the test green — and because
DQ-06 has never fired on real data, broken and working were indistinguishable. The predicate now
lives in `vw_DQ_OverlappingInstalls` and both call it.

**A fingerprint blind to everything that matters.** `CHECKSUM_AGG` aggregates by XOR, so 142 readings
cancelled to invisibility; and the hash omitted airframe, mission profile and date — the three
columns that decide which multiplier applies, which base it lands on, and which component accrues it.
A rebuild could have reassigned the entire fleet's workload and reproduced it exactly.

**A materialised optimisation, and the test that makes it safe.** A 57-threshold sweep took over ten
minutes because `vw_SensorFeatures` recomputed its windows on every pass. `dbo.AlertFrontier` keeps
only the points where a component's running-maximum vibration increases — 22,006 rows instead of
84,000 — and the sweep now takes **1.2 seconds**. UAT-35 recomputes the alert set the slow way and
requires it to match install for install and lead time for lead time, because a fast answer that
disagrees with the slow one is worse than the slow one.

Full write-ups in [docs/data_validation_report.md](docs/data_validation_report.md), including the
ten defects an adversarial review found that all 28 original tests passed over — and the one finding
in that review that turned out to be my own misreading.
