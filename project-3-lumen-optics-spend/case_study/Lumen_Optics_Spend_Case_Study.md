# The variance report is green and the money is gone
## An analyst case study — Lumen Optics Manufacturing photonics spend

**Reporting date:** 2025-12-31 · **Tooling:** T-SQL (SQL Server), Power BI (DAX), Excel

> **Data disclosure.** Lumen Optics Manufacturing is a fictional photonics manufacturer. Every
> figure in this study is synthetic, generated by the scripts in `sql/`, and reproducible from
> them. No confidential data is used and no claim is made about any production system.

---

## 1. The situation

Lumen Optics Manufacturing builds fibre-coupled laser modules. It buys around **$48.6M** a year
of direct materials — laser diodes, photodetectors, optical fibre, lens assemblies, coatings,
precision mounts, connectors and thermal control — from 36 suppliers across 140 part numbers.

Procurement reports purchase price variance monthly. It has been green all year: **−0.19%**,
meaning Lumen pays slightly *less* than its contracts require. Every category manager is hitting
their number.

Material cost per module has risen anyway. Nobody can say why.

### The question

> **Our purchase prices are flat or falling and every variance report is green — so why is
> material cost per accepted unit rising, and which supplier–part combinations should sourcing
> renegotiate first?**

---

## 2. Why the variance report cannot answer it

Purchase price variance compares what was paid against what was **agreed**. That makes it
structurally blind to the largest source of loss in a photonics book.

`✦` **Optical components follow learning curves.** As cumulative volume rises and processes
mature, unit costs fall — laser diodes at roughly 8% a year, connectors 7%, coatings 5%, fibre 4%.
This is not a forecast; it is how the category behaves.

A supplier who renews a contract **flat** while its category erodes 8% a year keeps everything the
curve was supposed to hand back. And because the price never *rose*, purchase price variance
records zero. Forever.

The measure that sees it is **erosion capture**: the share of the expected category decline that
actually showed up in the prices paid.

| | |
|---|---:|
| Expected erosion, spend-weighted across the book | benchmark |
| **Erosion actually captured** | **29.50%** |
| Target | 80.00% |
| **Annual value of the gap** | **$4,047,609** |

Lumen is collecting under a third of what its categories are giving away. That is roughly **8.3%
of annual direct-materials spend**, and no report in the business currently shows it.

---

## 3. Approach

### 3.1 One definition of what a purchase cost

```
LandedCost = OrderQty × UnitPrice + FreightAmount + ExpediteFee
CostPerAcceptedUnit = LandedCost ÷ QtyAccepted
```

Freight and expedite are held separately from the unit price on purpose: a unit price is
negotiated with a supplier, an expedite fee is caused by planning, and folding them together hides
which of the two is costing the money.

The second line is the one that changes decisions — see §4.2.

### 3.2 A price agreement is a dated fact, not an attribute

A contracted price is true between two dates and false outside them. Storing it on the part or the
vendor would let today's contract retroactively govern a purchase made two years ago, so every
historical variance would be measured against a price that did not exist at the time.

Two consequences the model has to handle explicitly:

- **An open-ended agreement is still in force.** Excluding `NULL` end dates is the usual way
  contract coverage is made to look far worse than it is. 1,333 lines here are governed by one of
  187 open agreements (UAT-03).
- **Overlapping agreements make the contracted price ambiguous.** 61 lines have two agreements in
  force at once. The resolution is deterministic by construction — latest start, then highest key
  — and the ambiguity is *reported* rather than silently resolved, because a variance computed off
  a coin-flip is a number with no error bar.

### 3.3 Every number computed twice

Each headline figure is implemented independently in T-SQL and again in Excel formulas, then
reconciled on the workbook's Validation sheet in the open. **All 14 checks agree**, and the
workbook says so on its own face.

## 4. What the data says

### 4.1 The erosion gap is concentrated, and it is where leverage is weakest

Of 185 vendor–part pairs with enough history to judge:

| Erosion band | Pairs | Spend (12m) | Share | Opportunity |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Beats the benchmark | 19 | $4,255,931 | 9.7% | — |
| Tracks it (60–100%) | 45 | $9,396,584 | 21.4% | $317,630 |
| Partial (20–60%) | 60 | $12,712,195 | 29.0% | $1,092,403 |
| **Flat (under 20%)** | **61** | **$17,495,221** | **39.9%** | **$2,637,575** |

**Two fifths of the spend sits with suppliers capturing less than a fifth of the expected
erosion.** But the sharper cut is by leverage:

| | Pairs | Opportunity | Avg leverage score | **Erosion captured** (spend-weighted) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Parts with 2+ qualified suppliers | 152 | $2,576,657 | 72.8 | **38.26%** |
| **Parts with one qualified supplier** | **33** | **$1,470,952** | **38.1** | **8.91%** |

> **Sole-source suppliers capture 8.91% of the expected erosion; competitive ones capture 38.26%.**
> Suppliers hold price precisely where Lumen cannot leave.

Both figures are **spend-weighted**, on the same basis as the 29.50% headline. An earlier draft of
this table quoted simple averages (37.77% and 10.88%), which are not comparable to the headline and
understated the gap — weighting by money widens it from 3.5× to 4.3×. Across 185 pairs ranging from
roughly $40,000 to several million in annual spend, an unweighted average lets a small pair count as
heavily as one carrying a hundred times the money.

That is not a surprise economically — it is what anyone would predict. What matters is that it is
now **quantified**: 36% of the total opportunity sits behind parts where the negotiating position
is half as strong, and treating those two populations identically in a sourcing plan wastes the
year.

### 4.2 The cheapest supplier is the most expensive one

VEN-004 undercuts every competitor on laser diodes. On part LOM-0072, three qualified suppliers:

| Vendor | Avg unit price | Acceptance | **Cost per accepted unit** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **VEN-004** | **$184.14** (cheapest) | 91.61% | **$204.71** |
| VEN-012 | $197.43 | 98.99% | **$204.44** ← cheaper |
| VEN-009 | $211.49 | 99.09% | $213.94 |

VEN-004's 6.7% price advantage is entirely erased by a rejection rate seven times its
competitors'. On this part it is not merely no cheaper — it is **dearer per usable component**.

A component that arrives outside specification was still paid for, still freighted, and cannot be
built into a module. Unit price cannot see any of that; cost per accepted unit is the only
comparison that can.

Across the book this costs **$1,571,168** of material paid for and unusable.

### 4.3 A quarter of the spend has nothing to negotiate against

**Maverick spend: 27.09%** — against a 5% target and a 12% warning.

The cause is not rogue buying; it is contracts quietly expiring. Roughly one agreement in seven is
never renewed, and purchasing simply continues at drifting spot prices. Maverick spend and the
erosion failure turn out to be the same story: a lapsed contract is a price nobody is watching.

There is nothing to renegotiate until there is something to renegotiate, which is why
`PUT_ON_CONTRACT` is a distinct action in the queue carrying **$1,623,978** of opportunity across
48 pairs.

### 4.4 Three metrics are green while a cohort inside them has failed

| Metric | Portfolio | Status | The cohort underneath |
|---|---:|:---:|---|
| Acceptance rate | 99.11% | **Green** | VEN-004 on Laser Diode: **91.4%** |
| On-time delivery | 80.62% | Red | VEN-009: **59.64%**, decayed from 83% through 2025 |
| Expedite spend | 0.23% | **Green** | Doubled between 2023 and 2025 on long-lead parts |

The acceptance figure is the instructive one. At vendor level VEN-004 sits at 98.3% and looks
unremarkable; only the **vendor × category** cut exposes 91.4%. A portfolio average cannot breach
until the whole book does, and by then the cohort that caused it has been failing for a year.

Every aggregate metric in this project therefore ships with its cohort cut attached to the same
report.

## 5. The operational control

185 vendor–part pairs is not a plan. A category manager can run perhaps eight serious negotiations
a quarter, so the queue stays complete for audit and marks what is actually workable.

**43 pairs this quarter, carrying 65.6% of the $4.05M opportunity.**

| Action | Pairs | Opportunity | Spend (12m) | This quarter |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `RENEGOTIATE` | 54 | $1,631,840 | $16,221,031 | 16 |
| `PUT_ON_CONTRACT` | 48 | $1,623,978 | $11,559,724 | 19 |
| `DUAL_SOURCE` | 8 | $515,330 | $4,652,024 | 8 |
| `ACCEPT` | 73 | $276,461 | $10,320,429 | 0 |
| `FIX_QUALITY` | 2 | — | $1,106,723 | 0 |

Four design decisions are worth naming:

- **Ranking is by money; the ACTION is by leverage.** The largest gaps sit on sole-source parts
  where there is no credible threat to make. Ranking on value alone sends a category manager into
  meetings they cannot win, which is the fastest way to lose a sourcing team's trust in a report.
- **Quality outranks price.** If the loss is rejected material rather than price, a price
  conversation is the wrong conversation. `FIX_QUALITY` routes those to a corrective action.
- **Above $100,000 a year, ask anyway.** Weak leverage is a reason to expect a hard conversation,
  not a reason to skip one. Before this rule existed, a $179k annual gap was classified `ACCEPT`
  because the supplier was large enough not to care.
- **`ACCEPT` is an honest answer.** 73 pairs worth $276k in total — averaging under $4k each —
  are parked with a reason. Saying so is more useful than listing them forever.

| # | Recommendation | Owner | Basis | Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Work the quarter's 43 pairs**, starting with the 19 `PUT_ON_CONTRACT` | Category managers | 43 pairs carry 65.6% of the total gap | **$2,657,185** of the $4.05M |
| **2** | **Re-paper the lapsed agreements** before negotiating anything on them | Sourcing ops | 27.09% of spend has no agreement in force | Unlocks **$1,623,978** that cannot be negotiated today |
| **3** | **Add erosion capture to the monthly pack**, beside purchase price variance | CPO | PPV reads −0.19% Green while $4.05M walks out | Makes the invisible loss visible monthly |
| **4** | **Move the laser diode volume off VEN-004** on shared parts | Optoelectronics | Cheapest per unit, dearest per *accepted* unit on every shared part | Part of the **$1,571,168** of unusable material |
| **5** | **Start a second source on the three largest sole-source parts** | Category managers | Sole-source capture is 8.91% against 38.26% where alternatives exist | Attacks **$1,470,952** where leverage is weakest |
| **6** | **Put VEN-009 on a delivery corrective action** | Supplier quality | On-time fell from 83% to 59.64% through 2025 | Removes the driver behind rising expedite fees |
| **7** | **Report every aggregate with its cohort cut** | Analytics | Acceptance is 99.11% company-wide and 91.4% for one vendor-category | Stops green lights hiding failing cohorts |
| **8** | **Resolve the 61 overlapping agreements** | Sourcing ops | Contracted price — and therefore PPV — is ambiguous on $1,495,917 of spend | Makes the variance figure meaningful |

Recommendations 2 and 3 cost nothing but a decision. Recommendation 1 is what the sourcing team
does on Monday.

## 7. What went wrong during the build

Five defects reached a working state before being caught. How each was found matters more than
that it existed.

### 7.1 A planted pattern that silently did not exist

The generator was told to make one named vendor the cheap-but-rejected laser diode supplier. But
vendor–part assignment is random, and that vendor was never assigned a single laser diode. The
`UPDATE` matched zero rows — **which does not raise an error**. It simply did nothing, and the
pattern was absent from the first profiling run.

Now the target is *derived* from the data (`the vendor supplying the most laser diode parts`) and
stored where both the price logic and the rejection logic read it.

### 7.2 A lapse that produced an eternal contract

Non-renewal is simulated by deleting the later contract terms. The surviving final term was then
marked open-ended — so a contract that **lapsed** became a contract that **never expires**, the
exact opposite of the pattern.

The tell was not an error: off-contract spend stayed at exactly 222 lines across two runs while
the lapse rate was more than doubled. A number that refuses to move when its driver changes is
evidence of a broken dependency.

### 7.3 A window function that saw one row

A pair-level erosion query reported *"185 of 185 vendor–part pairs holding price flat — 100%"*,
directly contradicting the category table above it, which showed prices falling.

The cause was `ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY …)` inside a `CROSS APPLY`: the window function
evaluates over the **single row** it was applied to, so every row received rank 1 both ascending
and descending, making first price and last price the same number.

A tidy way to produce a confident, plausible, entirely wrong headline.

### 7.4 Published figures that disagreed with their own components

`ExpectedPrice` was computed from an unrounded exponent while `YearsElapsed` was published rounded
to three decimals. A reader redoing the arithmetic from the printed numbers got a different answer
than the report. The same applied to erosion capture.

The rule now applied in SQL **and** in the DAX and Excel implementations: **a derived figure is
computed from its components as published, not from their unrounded intermediates.** UAT-05 and
UAT-07 assert it.

### 7.5 One defect reported twice, and one test that was simply wrong

Duplicated PO lines are inserted after receipts are generated, so they can never have a receipt —
and every one was also being reported as `NEVER_RECEIVED`, padding the defect count with a
consequence of something already on the report. Excluding them dropped the count from 114 to 94.

Separately, three UAT cases failed on their own premises rather than on the code: one compared a
rounded published figure against an unrounded recomputation, one compared committed spend against
received-only spend, and one held a constant that a later fix had moved. **The test failing does
not mean the code is wrong** — and the instinct to "fix" the code first is how a correct
implementation gets broken to satisfy a bad assertion.

---

## 8. What I would do differently

1. **Derive planted patterns from the data, never name them.** §7.1 cost a full profiling cycle,
   and the failure mode — a zero-row `UPDATE` — is silent by design in SQL.

2. **Treat "a number that did not move" as a finding.** §7.2 was caught only because I compared
   two runs. A generator change that does not change its output is either a no-op or a broken
   dependency, and both deserve investigation before the data is used.

3. **Build the should-cost model alongside the erosion model.** Erosion measures the failure to
   decline *from the first observed price*. It cannot say whether that opening price was fair — a
   part bought badly in 2023 and eroded perfectly since looks healthy here. That is a real
   limitation and the case for the complementary analysis, not a caveat to bury.

4. **Put the leverage cut in front of the opportunity ranking, not beside it.** The most useful
   single table in this study is §4.1's sole-source comparison, and it emerged from a review query
   rather than from the designed output. It should have been a headline from the start.
